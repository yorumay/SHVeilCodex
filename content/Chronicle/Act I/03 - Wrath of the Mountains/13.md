## Of the Fire in the Chamber, and of the Books That Were Not Burned

The thing in the frame came through burning at the edges of its own outline and said what it was and what it wanted. The monastery would burn and the world above it would follow. It called the woman in the ice by a foul name, and so they learned, before they had language for it, that she was not gone but only kept. It had been waiting a long while for its heart to be carried near enough that it might come the rest of the way; they had walked into the chamber a little before the finishing of what a decade of secret worship had prepared. Twice the fight nearly went otherwise, and two of them carry the marks of that room yet. When it fell the obsidian heart split along its length and shattered, and the pieces could not hold what had been bound in them. They went up and slept, not understanding what they had broken. In the morning the monastery was cold. The warmth that had held [[Hoshikage]] through every winter in living memory had stopped in the night, and the house had drawn down to its lowest floors to wait for a future no one could yet name.

They went back to the libraries and the upper floors were opened to them. The archivist of that house was old and quiet and looked at them longer than was polite before she chose to speak. The abbot had ordered the forbidden writings burned. She had not burned them; she had only moved them. What she unlocked was older than the monastery's own history and stranger than its present faith. [[Izuna]] had been wed. The monks of earlier centuries had prayed to his wife beside him and called her the [[Fuyukimi|Winter Maiden]], and the newer scriptures named that practice heresy. In the margin of a scroll no hand had touched in a decade, written by someone who had loved the subject: _She did not fall. He did._ Of [[Akujin]] there was nothing. He did not exist before the most recent decade. The brazier in the central church had been built in its first life as a machine — an artificer's marvel of a kinder century, made to draw warmth from the heart and carry it through the house. The worship came afterward, laid over the machinery by hands that knew it for a lie.

There was a fourth body of work, older than the monastery and perhaps older than the speech it was read in. It concerned the Hound. It told how his hunger turned at last upon faith itself, and how a chain was given him, and a wager made, and how his own arrogance became his leash; and it named among those who bound him three whose names are not written here together lightly. It ended in a hand that had pressed hard enough to score the page: _Let none approach the Chain. Let no voice speak his name in full where the stars touch the world. Let neither deity nor mortal seek the Hound, lest it remember the leash and awaken._ They wrote it all down. They closed the rooms and went to their beds. The windows broke before midnight. The defenders had held as long as men hold and had not been enough, and the snow-children were inside.
