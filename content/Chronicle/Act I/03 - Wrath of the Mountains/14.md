## Of the Prison and the Morgue, and of the Stair Beneath the Courtyard

The [[yuki-no-ko|snow-children]] in the corridors did not last long. They cut them down half-asleep and half-frozen and gathered what monks were still living into the central church, which was the only room still holding yesterday's heat in its stones, and slept where they could. The morning gave them back their feet but not the monastery; the upper levels had been taken in the night, and what remained of Hoshikage was rooms held with bolted doors and rooms given over to the dead. They went floor by floor, and what they found was older than the schism. An artificer's chamber held half-finished works no kinder century could account for. A diviner's cell had been given over by its last occupant to a single subject, pages upon pages of it, concerning the [[Kezef|Hound]]; the hand was patient and old and they had no hours to spare it. Another room held bodies — monks, travellers, beasts off the mountain — taken apart in a manner that resembled inquiry and was not. There was too much wrongness there to be read while the dead beat upon the doors, and they marked the rooms and left them.

The fourth floor of the old stone hall had been a prison and the fifth a morgue. The prisoners had been those monks who would not let go of the older faith and refused the writing of [[Izuna]] and his wife out of it, and they had been left in the cold to die. They were not in the cells. The bodies in the morgue, wrapped and bound, had been the first defenders to fall upon the palisade. They were not in the morgue. So it was understood that every one of the children walking that mountain had been a person whom someone in that house had failed. One cell stood apart from the others: hasty work, a wall raised in days where the stone had been bare, a door fitted afterward, and a single high window of stained glass already broken outward. In it they found the remainder of their lord's question. The monk who had come through impossible snow to [[Fukama]] and died upon the hall floor had begun from that cell. He had been named a doomsday-cultist and walled in. He broke the window with his hands, and went down four stories of icy stone, and walked out of the eye into the storm, and carried the words the length of the province with them in his sleeve.

The bells went again. The children had pressed through the courtyard and the great statue of [[Izuna]] had come down, and beneath it, where the stone had been dressed for centuries to look like ordinary flooring, a stair opened into the mountain. It was not the way the monks had taken to their new worship. It was older. Below, it came into a hall no living person had told them of, where supplicants had once prayed to the husband and the wife together before that was named heresy. At its centre stood a stone, smooth and cold and plainly older than the building above — the highest peak of the mountain, and the first place [[Izuna]] set foot when he came down out of the sky. The children walked past it without slowing. They were going deeper.
