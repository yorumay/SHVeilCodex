## Of the Forge at Kinzan, and of the Wolves in the Snow

Buckets and ladles served well enough. The [[mephit]]s broke into ice and steam where they fell, and they finished the night in the baths they had nearly died in, with the laughter gone out of the trees. Sleep came hard, and came. By morning the road was the road again.

At [[Kinzan]] they found the [[korobokuru]] child again. Months had passed since the temple in the ravine and she had not been idle. She was apprenticed to the smith [[Hagan]], called the one-armed flame, whose forge at [[Kinzan]] had outlasted three winters of that cold, and she had begun a blade. They left her at the anvil. West of [[Kinzan]] the road no longer existed; the snow had taken it weeks before they came looking. They cut their own line through the drifts, and [[Izunayama]] showed itself between the cloud-breaks and was gone again, near enough that the dark of its slopes could be read. The snow-children moved along that same line, ahead of them and beside them and sometimes around them, never turning, never closing.

The forest came up and the wolves came down. They were not hunting the six; they were hunting anything, and the six were what was there. Gaunt beneath their winter coats and hungry to the bone, they came in a pack that should not have been fed in a forest so cold. The fight was bad. While it was fought, a small line of the snow-children walked past the clearing and did not slow. When the last wolf fell they stayed where it had fallen, and built a fire, and butchered what could be butchered, and bound what could be bound, and let the storm have the rest of the day. [[Izunayama]] was still ahead.
