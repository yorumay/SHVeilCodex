## Of What Was Read in the Libraries, and of the Heart Beneath the House

The bells of the night before had been answered with steel; the morning was given to paper. They took the libraries that were open to them, the lower stacks and the second-floor reading rooms, and set about reading their way toward the prophecy. The day was long and the lamps burned thin. They learned more than they had asked for. [[Izuna]] came to them out of the older scrolls: celestial [[hengeyokai]], the founding chronicles called him, a weasel with a coat of starlight, born of the north wind and the spark of a first falling star. He had walked the heavens and had given divination to mortals — the sacred reading of stars, the making of prophecy. He had been patron of that house. The newer scrolls did not say his name. Some of the older murals had been painted over, and where the pigment lay thicker it could be seen that figures had once stood beside him. One was a pale woman. Her face was plaster now.

[[Akujin]] came to them out of newer scrolls, five years old, or ten at the most. The newer hand wrote of him as a fresh interpretation of the mountain's guardian — fire in the cold, warmth in despair, the flame in the brazier eternal — and called him another name for [[Izuna]], and the Mountain Flame. His murals were bright and lately made: a crowned figure of fire, and monks basking in the heat of him. In the central church an obsidian brazier was fed each day with coal out of the mountain's veins, and the younger monks called it his altar. Older work than any of it surfaced in the margins of scrolls they had not been looking for, in a hand old enough that the ink had browned. _Before the stars were set, before even the dream of shape, there was the [[Kezef|Hound]]. When the [[Takamagahara|First Light]] fell, it tore the silence, and from that tear the [[Kezef|Hound]] was born. Older than [[The Void|Void]], blacker than the shadows cast by the gods themselves._ They set the passages aside. They were not what the lord had sent them for. They wrote them down all the same.

Toward evening the abbot went from the upper hall alone and they followed at a distance, down into a level they had not been shown. The corridor ended in a chamber raw out of the mountain's stone, and at its centre, set in a frame of obsidian, was a heart. It pulsed, and it was warm. He knelt before it and prayed in a voice that was not the voice he used in his own hall, and when they came upon him he drew a dagger across his palm and set the bleeding hand against the stone. What followed happened without sound. He did not fall. He was unmade, drawn in through some opening the eye could not find, his whole body taken in an instant as a candle is pinched out. The heart took him. Then the frame darkened, and brightened, and held a shape: a figure of fire, with a crown of fire above its brow, looking through the chamber as a king looks through his hall.
