Three tellings, and in each of them a kami.

One had been loved and was forgotten, and went sour in the dark beneath a ravine, and had to be put down by the hands of people who did not know what they were killing. One was never a kami at all, but had been three hundred years at the work of becoming one, and had a village fed to her by its own consent. One had been a woman, and was made a storm by grief, and was let go of that storm by six strangers who chose to talk instead of draw. It was not recorded that any of the six noticed the pattern at the time. Those who tell it now put the three together on purpose, because it is the only place in the whole account where the question is asked plainly: what a kami is, and how one is made, and by whom.

This part is also where the six learn the country. They learn what a clan is owed and what a lord may ask. They learn that a village may prefer a monster it can live beside to strangers who would take it away. They learn the difference between a yokai and a kami and how narrow that difference sometimes is. Everything that follows depends on their having learned it, and it is worth saying that they learned most of it badly, and late, and by being wrong first.
