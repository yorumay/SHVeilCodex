## Of the First Night, as Those Who Lived Through It Tell It 

They say the fire came down upon the western quarter first and that the silver-hawk house stood in the middle of it, and that the six drew a line at the inner courtyard wall and held it with bucket-chains and with workings thrown to gut the flame short of the timbers. The house held. Many who defended it did not, and many of those who had kept it did not, and their names belong in a different place than this one. They say also that the six went out from it toward the castle while the quarter was still burning, and that this was the last anyone in that street saw of them for some hours.

What the city was, in those hours, no map since has agreed with. Streets that had been walked all that year were gone, or were wrong. Buildings stood where no building had stood. Buildings that had stood were not where they had been. People moved in the streets who should not have been moving, whom the fire had touched and not finished with, turned in the directions the fire pulled them; and things came out of alleys that had not been alleys the day before; and the dead came up through cellar doors and walked. It is told that the six cut a road through all of it and did not stop, and that more than one of them, looking down at what he had killed, knew the face of someone he had passed in the market that morning. Those who tell it do not name the faces. Some grief is not given to strangers on the night it is earned.

Above all this stood a shield of pale prismatic light about the Old Imperial Castle, visible from every quarter that still had a horizon, and every soul in [[Fukama]] that night looked toward it at least once. The lord's house held the only ward of that order in the province. It is said the six made for it and came to the canals, where a fortress sits upon an island in the moat, built in some forgotten reign to hold the approach; and that they stopped upon the bridge, because the fortress had grown old. The word is used exactly. Its mortar was crumbled and its tiles had lost their glaze and a flagpole that had stood straight that morning was bent at an angle no living wind could account for. In the light of the burning sky they were looking at a hundred years of weather laid on in the hours since sundown. They crossed, and put their backs to the doors, and pushed. Within, the lanterns were lit as lanterns are lit by hands that no longer notice the dark. The garrison had been thirty men at the last muster. Thirty turned to face them in the entry-hall wearing the garrison's armour and the garrison's faces, as the moon is the moon's face seen through fog — recognisably so, and not. The skin had pulled and the eyes had gone back and the teeth had grown. They had been men an hour before. One of them drew a blade.
