## Of the Eighty Years in the Fortress, and of the Coming of the Blessed Fire

The blade came down and they met it. The [[gaki]] fought as [[gaki]] fight, without strategy and without fear and without stopping, and they were put down, and the work was ugly, and the silence left in that entry-hall was not a silence the living should stand in long. Then came [[owlbear]]s, which are no beast this country has ever had — skeletal, the flesh long gone off their frames, the great clawed limbs moved by something that had judged bone sufficient. These also were put down. And a thing came up the canal for which the western texts have a word and this country has none, being the shifted dead grown together into one engine of bodies and walking upon the legs of all of them at once; and they fought it upon the bridge and won, and let the river take what was left. The night had opened borders that ought not to be opened, and was bringing things across them.

In the pause they read the fortress's papers, and this is the hardest matter in the account. The daily logs run in the captain's hand through the morning of the twenty-ninth of Tarsakh. After that hour the same hand continues, aged considerably. Other hands join it. The papers cover eighty years. The fire had taken the fortress out of its moat and set it elsewhere — _a forest,_ the older pages say, _dark, and without sun, and the night does not end here._ They held it. They defended the townsfolk who had been carried away with them. They lasted longer than men should. After some years the food ran out, and after some years more they turned to what could be turned to, and the late pages are honest in the way that only despair makes paper honest. They ate the inconsequential. They ate one another. So they endured eighty years by becoming the thing they had sworn never to become, and at the end of it the fire came again and set the fortress back into the canal, and they were killed upon their first night home. No judgement is offered here. None is owed.

They crossed onto the castle ground and joined the line at Tenku-jo, and the waves came down out of the broken sky and did not slacken, and the shield held, and the defenders held, and the castle held. Then the sky opened a second time, and what came through it was not the blue fire but light — the colour of a forge at its hottest, carrying the weight of a thing stored against that night since before the country was a country. The moat did not boil. It became lava. The walls lit from beneath and burned in colours stone does not burn in. And out of the lava rose [[Kagutsuchi]], whom his own father slew at his birthing: forty feet of magma and white fire, walking as one walks who has practised at having a body and not finished the practice. He turned his head, and his eyes found the ward upon the castle, and he did not look at the defenders at all. Then he opened his mouth, and what came out of it was his father's name. He had returned into the world to seek his father and his father's forgiveness, and that he came down confused and in the wrong place.
